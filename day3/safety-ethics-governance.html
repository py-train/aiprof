Using AI Responsibly

Hallucinations, bias, privacy, and governance — what every professional needs to know.

Hallucinations

The problem isn't that AI lies. It's that it doesn't know it's wrong.

A hallucination is when a model generates output that sounds confident and plausible — but is factually wrong. Not a glitch. Not a bug. A feature of how LLMs work.

LLMs don't retrieve facts from a database. They predict the most statistically likely next word given everything before it. The model has no internal fact-checker — no way to distinguish "I know this" from "this sounds right."

A real example

Ask an LLM to cite sources for a legal argument. It will produce citations — with author names, journal titles, volume numbers, page ranges — that look completely real. Many will not exist. The model isn't lying; it's pattern-matching what a citation looks like.

The danger isn't that AI is wrong. It's that it's wrong with complete confidence.

The more specific and obscure the topic, the higher the hallucination risk. General knowledge: usually fine. Niche regulations, specific case law, detailed financials: verify.

Managing hallucinations

Practical habits that reduce the risk

📎

Ground the model

Provide the facts yourself — paste in the document, policy, or data you want the model to work from. Ask it to answer only from that material. This is the principle behind RAG.

❓

Ask for uncertainty

Include in your prompt: "If you're not certain about something, say so." Models respond to this instruction — they'll flag low-confidence claims if you ask.

✅

Verify before you use

Treat AI output like a first draft from a knowledgeable but fallible colleague. Always check specific facts, dates, numbers, legal references, and named sources.

What not to do

Ask the model to check its own work on factual claims. It will often confirm the hallucination.

The right mental model: AI as a fast, fluent drafter — not an authoritative source.

Bias

The model reflects the world that trained it

LLMs are trained on enormous amounts of human-generated text. Human text contains human biases — around gender, race, culture, profession, and more. The model learns those patterns.

Where bias comes from

  • • Training data — over-represents certain languages, cultures, and viewpoints
  • • RLHF — human raters have their own preferences and blind spots
  • • Fine-tuning — can amplify existing biases in a narrow domain

How it surfaces

  • • Stereotyping — associates certain roles or traits with particular groups
  • • Uneven quality — significantly stronger in English and Western contexts
  • • Over-refusal — refuses legitimate requests that pattern-match to something sensitive
  • • Subtle framing — describes the same scenario differently depending on demographic cues

Bias in AI is the bias in us, amplified and automated.

Treat bias as a known limitation to manage — not an argument to avoid AI entirely.

Bias (contd.)

What to do about bias

🔬

Test with diverse examples

Before deploying AI for any use case that affects people, test it with varied inputs — different names, contexts, demographic framings. Does quality stay consistent?

🧑‍⚖️

Never use AI alone for high-stakes decisions

Never use AI output as the sole basis for decisions about individuals — hiring, performance management, credit, or medical triage. Always involve human judgment.

📋

Frame it as a known limitation

Redirect "is AI biased?" to "how do we test for and mitigate known biases in this deployment?" It's an engineering and process problem, not a reason to avoid AI entirely.

IP & copyright

Who owns AI-generated output?

This is genuinely unsettled territory. The law is still catching up, and the answer varies by jurisdiction.

Training data

  • • Most frontier models were trained on internet text, including copyrighted material
  • • Multiple lawsuits are active (NYT vs. OpenAI; Getty vs. Stability AI)
  • • Outcome: unresolved — especially for verbatim reproduction

AI-generated output

  • • In most jurisdictions, AI-generated content has no automatic copyright protection
  • • A human who substantially shapes or edits AI output may hold copyright on the result

Safe practices

  • • Don't pass AI-generated content off as unaided original work — especially for client deliverables, publications, or academic submissions
  • • Use AI as a drafting tool; apply human judgment, editing, and review
  • • Follow your organization's disclosure requirements

Think of it like using stock photography: AI content is a tool, not a credential.

If you wouldn't email it to a stranger, don't paste it into a public AI tool.

Governance

Governance isn't bureaucracy — it's risk management

Why it exists

  • • Liability — AI-generated advice or decisions that cause harm can expose the organization legally
  • • Quality — without standards, outputs vary wildly in tone, facts, and format
  • • Trust — clients, regulators, and employees need assurance AI is used responsibly
  • • Compliance — sector rules (financial advice, medical, legal) impose constraints AI doesn't know about

The governance spectrum

No policy Risky — common in early adoption
Informal norms Most organizations today
Approved tools list Emerging best practice
Formal AI policy Mature — standard in regulated industries

Policy elements

Common elements of an organizational AI policy

Policy elementWhat it means in practice
Approved tools / modelsA defined list of AI products employees may use (e.g., Copilot M365, approved API access)
Data classification rulesWhich data classes may be processed by which tools (public ✅, confidential ⚠️, restricted ❌)
Human-in-the-loop requirementsCertain outputs must be reviewed by a qualified human before use (legal, medical, HR decisions)
Disclosure requirementsWhen to disclose that AI was used (client deliverables, publications, regulatory submissions)
Incident reportingHow to report unexpected or harmful AI outputs
Training requirementsWho needs to complete AI literacy training before using approved tools

Know your organization's policy before you need it — not after something goes wrong.

Decision framework

Before you use AI for a work task — five questions

1

Is this tool approved?

Use only tools on your organization's approved list. When in doubt, ask IT or compliance.

2

What data am I inputting?

If it's confidential, restricted, or personally identifiable — use an enterprise tool with a DPA, or don't use AI at all.

3

Will a human review this?

High-stakes outputs — client advice, employment decisions, regulatory filings — need human review. Always.

4

Does accuracy matter here?

If yes: verify specific facts, numbers, and references before using the output.

5

Am I representing this as my own?

Be transparent about AI involvement as required by your organization's policy and professional standards.

Using AI to draft an internal meeting summary, then reviewing and editing it✅ Good use
Pasting a client's full contract into ChatGPT to get a quick summary⚠️ Risky — confidentiality breach
Using AI to generate a performance review and submitting it without reading❌ Clearly bad — no human judgment

Takeaway

Responsible AI use is a professional skill

AI isn't going away. The question isn't whether to use it — it's how to use it well.

What responsible use looks like

  • • You understand the tool's limitations (hallucinations, bias, knowledge cutoff)
  • • You protect confidential information as a habit
  • • You apply human judgment to any output that matters
  • • You follow your organization's guidelines and update your knowledge as they evolve
  • • You speak up when you see AI used badly — by yourself or others

What it doesn't look like

  • • Treating AI output as ground truth
  • • Pasting anything and everything into public tools
  • • Hiding AI use when disclosure is expected
  • • Assuming the organization has figured out policy so you don't have to

The professionals who use AI well aren't the ones who trust it most. They're the ones who know exactly where it fails.
