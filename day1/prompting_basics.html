Prompting Basics

From first prompt to structured reasoning patterns

Starting point

What a prompt really is

Not just a question

A prompt is a task specification: what you want, for whom, under what constraints, in what format, and how success should be judged.

A good rule of thumb

If a new team member would struggle to do the task from your instructions, the model likely will too.

Core components of a strong prompt

1

Context

Background, domain, audience, source material, and purpose.

2

Task

The action you want: summarize, extract, draft, compare, classify, plan.

3

Constraints

Tone, length, format, exclusions, boundaries, and business rules.

4

Evaluation

What “good” looks like: accuracy, readability, traceability, completeness, safety.

Formula

A beginner-friendly prompt template

You are helping with [role / situation].
Task: [what to do].
Context: [background / source / audience].
Constraints: [tone, length, rules, do / do not].
Output format: [bullets / table / email / JSON].
Quality bar: [what to optimize for].

Bad → Good → Better → Best

Bad

“Write an HR email.”

Good

“Write an HR email announcing 4 days office attendance.”

Better

“Write a professional company-wide HR email explaining 4 days in office from next month.”

Best

“Draft a concise, empathetic HR email for all employees announcing 4 days in office from next month and mandatory AI training completion by quarter end. Include subject line, rationale, key actions, and a supportive closing.”

Why better prompts work better

🎯

Less ambiguity

The model wastes less effort guessing what you mean.

🧭

Stronger steering

Format, tone, and audience cues narrow the response space.

✅

Easier review

A structured output is faster to check, edit, and reuse in workflows.

Prompting styles

Zero-shot and few-shot

Zero-shot

Summarize this meeting note in 5 bullets for a senior manager.

Use when the task is common and the format is straightforward.

Few-shot

Example input: messy note
Example output: action list
---
Now do the same for this new note.

Use when you want the model to imitate structure, tone, or labeling style more reliably.

Chain-of-thought and Tree-of-thought

CoT

Encourages stepwise reasoning. Best when the task benefits from breaking the problem into ordered steps.

Solve this step by step.
First identify the assumptions,
then compare options,
then recommend one.

ToT

Encourages exploring multiple candidate paths before choosing a direction. Useful for planning, strategy, and creative alternatives.

Generate 3 solution paths.
Evaluate trade-offs of each.
Then choose the best option.

When to use which style

Zero-shot for simple drafting and formatting

Speed

Few-shot for consistent style and structure

Reliability

CoT for reasoning-heavy tasks

Traceability

ToT for planning and option exploration

Depth

Iteration

Prompting is usually multi-turn

Turn 1

Get a rough first draft and expose what is missing.

Turn 2

Refine the brief: add missing audience, tone, facts, and format requirements.

Turn 3

Critique the output, request revisions, and add validation criteria.

Evaluate outputs, not just prompts

Accuracy

Are the facts right?

Completeness

Did it cover the required pieces?

Usefulness

Can someone actually use this result?

Risk

Could the output be harmful, misleading, or overconfident?

Instruction tuning

Why models respond to prompts at all

Base model

Learns language patterns by predicting tokens over very large corpora.

Instruction-tuned model

Further trained on examples of instructions and desired responses, so it becomes more helpful, structured, and obedient to task framing.

A tiny instruction-tuning example

{
  "instruction": "Summarize this policy update for employees in 5 bullets.",
  "input": "Starting next month, employees are expected to work from office 4 days per week...",
  "output": "- Office attendance becomes 4 days per week from next month.\n- Mandatory AI training must be completed by quarter end.\n- Managers should support transition planning.\n- Employees should review schedule expectations.\n- HR will share follow-up guidance if needed."
}

How prompting techniques shaped today’s systems

Prompt templates

Drove reusable workflows in copilots, chat assistants, and enterprise automation.

Few-shot examples

Made style transfer, extraction, and structured outputs far more repeatable.

Reasoning scaffolds

Inspired agent planning loops, tool routing, self-critique, and multi-step workflows.

Practical guardrails

Five habits to teach every beginner

1. Give the model the job, audience, and goal.
2. Ask for a specific format you can inspect quickly.
3. Use examples when consistency matters.
4. Challenge the output: ask what is missing, uncertain, or risky.
5. Verify important facts before using the result.

Bridge to practice

Good prompting is clear thinking made visible

When participants learn to add context, task, constraints, examples, and evaluation, they are not “tricking” the model — they are specifying work more intelligently.
